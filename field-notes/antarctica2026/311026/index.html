FIELD NOTES / ANTARCTICA 2026

The Antarctica Diaries - Departure Day

Field Date:
31 October 2026
Location:
Cape Town to Novo Station
Conditions:
-10°C / -14°C, windspeed 20-40 kph
Status:
Packed and prepped to go!

Well today is the day! I am headed to Antarctica!

It was an early morning, made more early by the fact that I’d stayed up very late the night before, downloading any possible code, manual or computer program I thought I might need in Antarctica. And a good thing too! Turns out the internet situation wouldn’t be quite as I’d hoped, but more on that later.

I’m travelling down to Antarctica with the fifth member of our expedition, Arianna Crosta, a fellow postdoc from the University of Innsbruck. The rest of our expedition team, Dale Andersen (Expedition Lead, SETI Institute), Miles Ecclestone (Camp Manager, Trent University), and Alex Cuzzeri (Doctoral student, University of Innsbruck) are already at the base awaiting our arrival. We were picked up at 7am sharp by Ultima, the South African-based logistics company that manages all of SETI’s Untersee Activities. Two days previous, we’d dropped off our large expedition duffle bags to Ultima to be preloaded onto the aircraft, containing everything we would be carrying down to Antarctica, mine crammed with everything from clothing to toolkits to spare robot parts to emergency chocolate, vegemite and of course, a hearty Earl Grey tea stash that would make Jean Luc Picard proud. We were also required to pack a small ‘polar bag’, that will come with us onto the flight, stuffed with a full base, mid and outer layer that can withstand polar temperatures, to be donned just before landing. We’d been living out of our small carry ons for the last few days in Cape Town, and would carry our own backpacks and in my case, robotic equipment, onto the plane ourselves. The night before the flight, my robotic instrument was safely loaded into her pelican case, sequestered in bubble wrap, each component carefully disassembled and individually packed, using a combination of the one hex key I left unpacked, a plastic knife from a nearby takeout place, and some nail clippers (never be without some tools!). Battery packs, camera, Garmin InReach and phone all charged.

Arriving at Cape Town International Airport, we were issued with our Antarctic boarding pass, and yes, we still have to go through passport control. A quick last minute hot chocolate and enough time for a final phone call home to say bye, and we’re loaded onto the aircraft!

It takes about 5.5 hours to fly from Cape Town to Novolazarevskaya (Novo) Station, our temporary home for the next few days while we pack up the gear into snow mobile sleds. Several weeks earlier, the Blue Runway was prepared, snow cleared and ice smoothed, to allow our aircraft to make a smooth touchdown at approximately 1:30pm local (local in Antarctica being UTC, as we’re sitting at about 11.5 degrees of Longitude at Novo). We’re flying with a true kaleidoscope of Antarctic people. Novo Station and Ultima Air Base are a bit of a way station for all manner of people who find themselves in Antarctica for the summer season - seasonal workers (cooks, mechanics, truck drivers - the people who make Antarctiuca run) heading off to the various camps in this part of the continent, researchers making their way to various stations to start their summer science research programs, experienced Antarctic guides who were setting off for some true exploring in the Eastern part of the continent to spend the next month setting up weather stations, skiing and mountaineering to find safe routes and potential campsites for Ultima, and paying tourists, who fly down to spend a few nights or weeks at Ultima’s beautiful Oasis camp and experience Antarctica for themselves.

The flight truly feels like something out of a film, we are all crammed into the back of the plane, in utalitarian seats, our polar bags piled in the back part of the cabin. Its so loud you can’t really hear anyone (sound cancelling hadphones were a true godsend at this point), but we got a delicious final meal of South African Bolivie, and hot drinks. The cabin is kept toasty warm until 1 hour before landing. At this point we’re instructed to donn our polar clothing, as the plane reaches the edge of the Antarctic continent. I suppose the best way to describe this stage is shear f****g chaos. A human conveyor belt forms down the central aisle, and polar bags are hauled along to the front of the plane, everyone eagled-eyed trying to spot their small duffle in a sea of duffles. As bags gets distributed, all 60 people in the back of the plane, in seats the same size as a commercial airline, with about as much leg room between them, attempt to strip down to their undies and put on thermal base layers, mid layers fleeces, puffy jackets, and hardy outer shells, as well as beanies, buffs, mitts and glacier goggles. The Captain blessedly turns down the heat, and through the two tiny windows at the front of the cabin, extremely bright sunlight can be seen illuminating the cabin, sunlight reflecting on the vast icescape below us. A screen at the front is showing us the pilot’s view - fractured sea ice slowly giving way to a great solid white expanse as far as the eye can see. We’ve arrived in Antarctica.

There is a palpable energy in the cabin, emnanting especially off those of us for whom this is still a novelty. The more experienced hands, people on their third, fourth, or in some cases eighth season, are decidely more chill, sitting back in their seats with their headphones on, eyes closed. But certaintly for Ari and myself, we were bouncing in our seats, phones out to eagerly film the descent onto the ice runway - just as smooth as you’d expect.

The chaos of the earlier polar clothing change is replicated in the disembarkation. Ari and I hang back, I don’t want to risk my equipment being damaged in the chaos. The 35-person Chinese research team file off in matching orange outfits, the tourists excitedly snapping pictures as they depart, then the ‘locals’, with their well-worn, much repaired gear that has seen many a season down here. Finally Ari and I step off the plane, into a truly blinding environment (thank god for glacier goggles!). Across the blue ice runway you can see scattered cargo containers, various snow clearing vehicles and snow mobiles zipping here and there picking up passengers, “ONA-SETI?” a clipped Africaans accent calls? “Thats us!” And then three very exciting words … “Welcome to Antarctica!”
